# Document Structures

A structure is scaffolding for the reader's understanding, not a form to force content into.

- Clarify the document's job: decide, inform, persuade, teach, or entertain — the job suggests the shape.
- Match structure to audience attention: executives get the conclusion first, learners get
  prerequisites first, reference readers get stable lookup sections.
- Useful skeletons to draw on: pyramid (answer, then supporting arguments), journey (problem,
  attempts, resolution), field guide (one topic per section with examples), and Q&A.
- For longer works, outline to a depth where every heading still carries a distinct promise;
  merge sections that make the same promise.
- Front-load a summary or TL;DR whenever the reader may stop after the first screen.
- Keep one idea per paragraph and make headings informative ("Costs drop 30 % after migration"
  rather than "Results").
- Plan the artifacts early: tables, figures, checklists, and appendices often carry the load
  better than more prose.
- When the user provides a template or prescribed format, follow it exactly.

These are options, not requirements. The user's purpose and format preferences always win.
